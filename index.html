I built this thing because my digital archives are an embarrassment! 30 years of getting a new hard drive whenever capacity quadrupled and dragging the old one to it into a folder titled "BACKUP - TO SORT" will do that to you lol, especially when you know deep down that "sorting" will never happen. To try to deal with that growing mess on a daily basis, I've tried a patchwork of all of our favorites in the past -- Everything, grep, custom scripts, you name it. And don't get me started on built-in OS defaults like Windows Explorer, Finder, Spotlight. I figured it was time to make something better-suited to my tastes, and 4200 commits later here we are!

RCHIVE scans as many different sources as you throw at it, gives you unified deep search across the entire catalog (with drives connected or disconnected), allows you to virtually organize and clean up giant archives of digital stuff by applying recipes and transformations, resiliently copies or moves files when you're ready to actually restructure on disk, or just lets you quickly pop in to find something or browse everything with previews, metadata, etc.

It works on Windows, Mac or Linux, works offline, has zero telemetry, deletes nothing, and is fully deterministic in operation. So, no wacky AI integrations for decision making or classification, everything is predictable and repeatable, you can even run portable versions off of a USB drive if you don't want to install it.

I've been using it all day every day for a year and am just stoked to have it out there, get some users, get some feedback (good or bad), and see what people think. There's a free trial, no email address or anything needed.

The website is also full of videos, features, comparisons, all that stuff, as well as a ncdu-like companion app for figuring out where the hell all your disk space went.

Anyways, check it out! Should come in handy if you're ever in Grand Central trying to find a garbage file left on some dude's Gibson.

GPT-6.2 Astra is the first model to beat Googlers' code golf records

2026-11-22 @ 16:44:48Points: 163Comments: 35

11 years later, LLMs are evolving fast. A recent OpenAI post ("Perplexity and Intelligence", https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/) described researchers watching a model solve open math problems one after another in a Slack thread. I started to wonder: how would frontier models compete with the records that a small group of brilliant minds created with hours of effort each week?

So I built this benchmark. Frontier models do find a lot of new approaches (and very brilliant ones) to solve these problems. For example, in two problems ("Surviving Queens" and "The Rolling Dice"), frontier models use complex numbers to solve them in Python and Ruby (both languages have built-in complex number support). And for the "(-5)-Smooth Numbers" problem, where you have to write code to list the first n positive integers of the form (-2)^x⋅(-3)^y⋅(-5)^z, most solutions build them with nested loops or by growing a set, then sort. GPT-6.2 Astra gets them all from powers of 4, and earns a sole best with this brilliant solution: 

    g=lambda n:sorted(4**i%(2**999-5)%(2**49-3)for i in range(7000))[:n]
There is a dedicated section for this: https://bjin.github.io/go-codegolf-benchmark/#why/finding-a-...

It's nice to see GPT-6.2 Astra become the first frontier model to beat the human records in code golfing. Along with other frontier models, it finds a lot of interesting solutions that Googlers back in 2015 didn't. On the other hand, I also believe code golfing can be a good benchmark (not a comprehensive one, of course) for frontier models.

Code golfing rewards thinking differently, because a shorter solution usually comes from looking at the problem from a different angle. It also rewards running experiments, not just thinking: writing a search program to find a shorter expression, or exploring lesser-known features and functions in the target compiler, interpreter, or library. Human code golfers do this a lot, and no public training data tells a frontier model how to do so.
