Launch HN: Versine (YC F26) – MCP to send and receive money

2026-11-24 @ 20:44:15Points: 13Comments: 5

https://versine.com). Versine lets you send and request money just by talking or texting with your AI (think Venmo with an MCP). We’re software engineers who have worked on distributed systems at Stripe, Databricks, and Amazon.

Versine is an MCP that sits in front of a wallet with a balance. With it, your agent can do things like:

- Send money to someone with your approval (via passkey in our app)

- Send and request money to/from up to 100 people at once

- Send money to people without a Versine account (they cash out to their bank in 2 mins)

- Pay people without your explicit approval to trusted contacts (you can set this)

Here’s a demo: https://youtube.com/shorts/Q9X7zioEqi0

We started working on this because assistants are frustratingly hands-off when it comes to finance and money movement. You can have your assistant go through a receipt and map out all the amounts owed taking care of splitting the subtotal, taxes, tips, but at the end of it the assistant hands back work to you when you have to go out and make the Venmo transfer.

Our current solution is oriented towards power users: those who want to automate everything through their agent use us to split dinners, group trips, or settle poker ledgers. We see ourselves unlocking more value for the general consumer as

1. More people turn into power users of agents, and 2. We expand to a more complete banking solution: deposits, transfers, wires, everything you can (and more) do with your bank offered as an MCP.

Behind the scenes, each user has a self-custodial stablecoin wallet storing USDC on Base. With self-custodial wallets, users own their keys, meaning money can’t be sent without their approval.

Interestingly, we were almost forced into this solution because we had around 4 weeks to build this product (after a pivot), and traditional fiat with a partner bank would have caused far too many regulatory and compliance slowdowns.

Because of that, the only fast option that remained was using stablecoin wallets. It also ensures we aren't a Money Services Business (MSB) since we don’t hold control or access to the user’s funds.

Each call to the wallet creates what we call one-time or recurring standing permissions. For a one-time payment, your agent makes an MCP call, and you receive a notification in our mobile app. Once you swipe up in the app and approve with a passkey (or your device’s biometric method), we create a one-time permission that instructs your wallet to send $X to your recipient’s address.

Trusted contacts let your agent send money to people that you’ve previously authorized without explicit passkey approval. Generally, these are people that you’d trust to send your money back even if your agent made a mistake.

We’re also able to support sending money to someone off Versine using claim forwarding, where the user authorizes us to forward a specific amount to a future address (which we create once we verify a recipient's OTP).

Param worked on scaling vector search at Databricks and harness evals at Glean. Harshith worked on core Stripe Connect fee-processing infrastructure and built an AWS Fargate autoscaler at Amazon. Vishnu worked on intent-based agent testing infrastructure at Amazon. None of us were experienced in stablecoin or crypto infra before working on Versine but our distributed systems experience has helped us a lot.

We'd love for you guys to try it! It's available on iOS and Android and as an MCP on ChatGPT and Claude. We also support a public MCP and REST API for other agents.

Please let us know how you find it and if it works as you'd hoped!

Show HN: Mason – A lightweight Postgres read-replica, built on columnar storage

2026-11-24 @ 16:11:52Points: 60Comments: 9

So, this started out as: let’s just connect Clickhouse to our postgres dbs and throw our analytics queries at it instead. That will speed everything up. But we quickly found that 1) Clickhouse comes with quite the learning curve (engine optimisation) to make it fast (we needed something we could easily drop in to any customer’s postgres scenario), and 2) we had underestimated how different clickhouse’s SQL syntax was to postgres. Don’t get me wrong: we love Clickhouse (it’s ridiculously fast if you know what queries to expect), it just didn’t work for our ad-hoc postgres workloads.

So we went searching for alternatives that were 1) more plug-and-play and 2) looked a lot more like postgres.

We dismissed a bunch based on desk research alone:

- pg_mooncake (seems to have stalled since data bricks bought them)

- pg_duckdb (seems to offer fast queries, or fresh data, but not both - we needed both)

- materialize/risingwave/readyset (like Clickhouse, they require knowing the query shapes upfront - not ideal for ad-hoc workloads)

- firebolt (license makes it challenging to offer to our customers)

- redshift / alloydb / etc (we wanted something cloud-provider agnostic)

And we shortlisted 3:

- pg_clickhouse (Postgres extension that parses SQL in Postgres and pushes the work down to ClickHouse)

- cedarDB (closed-source, Postgres-compatible analytical database)

- duckdb (open-source, embedded columnar analytics engine with a Postgres-like SQL dialect)

We needed to know how they would perform on real postgres queries, so we took a backlog of 5,410 (hand-written) queries (chosen because they had been slow to execute against postgres) from 159 real postgres databases. We then generated synthetic data (dynamically using the schemas from those 159 databases) to fill 159 local databases so we could run the experiment.

We then spun up the 3 short-listed tools locally, and ran each of the 5,410 queries against both postgres and the 3 tools, and compared the results:

- pg_clickhouse (matched postgres 59.4% of the time, refused 39.9% of the queries and was “wrong” 0.8% of the time).

- cedarDB (matched postgres 88.1% of the time, refused 8% of the queries and was “wrong” 3.9% of the time).

- duckdb (matched postgres 84.1% of the time, refused 14.3% of the queries and was “wrong” 1.6% of the time).

Matched meant they returned exactly the same result as postgres. Refused meant they returned an error message (usually telling you some syntax isn’t recognised). And “wrong” meant they didn’t return the same results as postgres. I appreciate that arguably postgres may be the one that is wrong in some cases - but for our use case, we considered postgres the golden source of truth.

So, both duckdb and cedardb performed pretty well (correctly handled more than 80% of the postgres queries we threw at it), out-of-the box. But we really still wanted to try and cover that long-tail.

We chose duckdb as its open-source license would allow us to easily work on the long tail to get the postgres SQL parity we wanted.

So, Mason (linked above), is what we built, powered by duckdb, which now matches postgres on 96% of our 5,410 queries, plus hundreds of other queries that we added, testing it with Metabase, Grafana, Superset, DBeaver and Trevor.io. For the remaining 4%, it errors with a nice clear message telling you how to rewrite your SQL.

We’d love your feedback on what we’ve built. Useful? How can we make it better?

Show HN: Brickie – Deploy sandboxes in your own AWS account

2026-11-24 @ 15:48:34Points: 14Comments: 0

https://github.com/hatchet-dev/hatchet). We’re releasing a new open-source project today called Brickie. Brickie lets you run sandboxes in your own cloud account, primarily targeting EKS on AWS.

Sandboxes are an incredibly useful primitive for agentic applications. They serve two main functions: (1) a way for the agent to safely execute untrusted code, and (2) a durable filesystem for agents to preserve their state via snapshots.

We decided to start building this after we started on a package called `@hatchet-dev/sandbox` to integrate more directly with various sandbox providers, like E2B, Daytona, Archil, and Modal. We discovered that a lot of users had a requirement to self-host their sandbox infra for part of (or all of) their stack, due to a self-host requirement for enterprises. We searched around for the easiest way for users to self-host a sandbox stack and agent-sandbox (https://agent-sandbox.sigs.k8s.io) was the closest thing to what we had in mind: it’s a kubernetes-sigs project implementing a Kubernetes operator which spawns sandboxes as pods on supported isolation layers like Firecracker, gVisor, and Kata Containers. We learned that many platform teams were adopting this because it's a standard k8s-based deployment model and is pretty easy to customize the isolation layer, network config, etc.

However, it was missing some necessary features. brickie builds on top of the agent-sandbox project and extends it to support proper sandbox (filesystem-based) snapshots, fork, pause, and resume, all while bundling a proper orchestration and observability layer via Hatchet.

Zooming out a bit, Hatchet is an orchestration engine which supports durable execution (https://hatchet.run/blog/durable-execution). Throughout 2026, we saw high adoption from users building agents using durable workflows as the statefulness layer and execution model. But as sandboxes became more popular, something didn’t sit right with us. Sandboxes _also_ provide a durability layer, but at the filesystem level, and it became important to disambiguate what we call session state from filesystem state. I wrote a lot more about this here: https://hatchet.run/blog/agent-statefulness.

The problem with having state in multiple places is that it needs coordination, which adds complexity to your application. This was part of the motivation behind `@hatchet-dev/sandbox`: we wanted to provide an easy way to take coordinated snapshots of agent state, even when using an external sandbox provider.

So when we started looking at self-hosted sandbox options, it was critical that this architecture was supported well. We decided to support this in brickie using a pretty simple snapshotting mechanism which tars and stores snapshots in an object store (S3). This is something we’re actively working to improve and make more performant.

Brickie is early and in beta, we're looking for testers, contributors, and feedback on the deployment model. Thanks in advance!

Show HN: KradleBench – Multi-agent AI evals in Minecraft

2026-11-23 @ 07:39:04Points: 9Comments: 2

This is James and Kemal. In past lives we led Firebase and Tensorflow/JAX respectively. HN has been a particularly big part of my (James’) career and the trajectory I’ve taken would be meaningfully different without it. Thank you!

Our team shipped a benchmark: KradleBench. We test AI in Minecraft Worlds.

1. The worlds are multi-agent. Humans and other AIs are in there too, often pursuing their own goals. Collaboration and conflict skills fall out of this property.

2. The world are dynamic. They constantly change, which means that agents need to react. “Capture the Flag” has a single winning condition, and many different ways to achieve it. This leads to many emergent and creative strategies.

3. The worlds are real-time. They are not turn-based, they do not wait for AIs to finish thinking. Nature balanced speed and intelligence in humans, we can understand this in AIs too.

Underneath all this is Kradle, a platform to test and understand intelligence. It’s a petri dish where any intelligence type (LLM, World Model, Human) can be compared and generate useful information to improve current systems, whether it’s on capabilities or safety.

Some findings so far: - A skilled human was #1, until yesterday when Astra 6.2 surpassed them. We lose badly on solo tasks and out-compete on multi-agent ones - Astra 6.2 iterates on its strategy 4x faster than Fable 5.2. The Claude family tends to think hard and stick with strategies. The OpenAI models tend to be more reactive and iterative, which serves them well in multi-agent, dynamic, real-time evals.

We’ve got videos on all the top model’s page, and we’ll be producing them when a new model launches. Sign up on the site to get them and for when the platform opens.
